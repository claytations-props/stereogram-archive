Mauch Chunk, the town that sold coal, then sold the ride

This is a research note, separate from the catalogue records. It does not change any catalogue title, place, date, or blurb. Cards listed at the end are illustration only.

The short version

Mauch Chunk was built to move coal. When a better coal route arrived, the old gravity railroad did not die. It became the attraction, and the town was marketed as the “Switzerland of America.” The railroad outlived its freight job by decades, then closed in the Depression. The town later lost even its name.

Coal first

The Lehigh Coal and Navigation Company was formed in 1818 by Josiah White and Erskine Hazard. Its problem was moving coal from Summit Hill about nine miles to Mauch Chunk on the Lehigh River, where it could be loaded for Easton and Philadelphia.

Ground was broken for the Gravity Railroad on January 1, 1827, and the line from Summit Hill to Mauch Chunk was completed by May the same year. Loaded cars ran downhill by gravity at 96 feet per mile. Mules originally returned the empties.

That is from the National Register nomination for the Mauch Chunk and Summit Hill Switchback Railroad, entered June 3, 1976, which also lists the line as 8.5 miles between Summit Hill and Mauch Chunk, now Jim Thorpe.

The switch to tourism

The National Canal Museum’s Switchback ephemera post puts the turn plainly: by 1870, the railroad was no longer the main means of transporting coal, but it had become a popular tourist destination. Mauch Chunk had developed a reputation as the “Switzerland of America,” with settings described as ideal for tourists escaping dirty, crowded East Coast cities.

The nomination gives the mechanism behind that turn. By 1872, the Hauto Tunnel provided a more efficient means for transporting coal. By 1874, the Central Railroad of New Jersey purchased the railroad and leased it to local residents who operated it for the many tourists who visited. The nomination’s summary is that Mauch Chunk became a tourist mecca for residents of the urban areas of the East Coast.

The 1921 season shows how routine that tourism had become. The Canal Museum describes a ticket for one trip, a timetable with six excursions per day lasting just under 90 minutes, and trolley cars from the Carbon County Courthouse to the Switchback depot. Riders were hoisted to 1,500 feet at Mt. Pisgah, paused at High Point, rode six miles down, and were hoisted again over 1,600 feet to Summit Hill, where they could visit surface mining operations and the “burning mines” that caught fire in 1859.

The end, with dates kept straight

Do not collapse the end into one year. The sources found so far distinguish three steps:

  • Last cars ran in October 1933, according to Lehigh Gorge Scenic Railway history, which also notes only 139 riders on Independence Day 1932.
  • The line was sold for scrap in 1937. Clio dates the purchase to September 2, 1937, by Isaac Weiner for $18,100. The Switchback Gravity Railroad Foundation agrees it ceased operations in 1933 and its metal parts were sold for scrap in 1937.
  • The National Register nomination says by 1937 the line was abandoned, primarily because the Central Railroad of New Jersey no longer ran excursions.

Wikipedia’s shorthand “operated until 1932” should not be used alone. It appears to mark a different endpoint than the last runs.

The name that disappeared

In 1954, the communities of Mauch Chunk and East Mauch Chunk merged to form the borough of Jim Thorpe, according to Britannica. That is the basic merger and rename fact used here. The fuller story of the negotiations, and what was promised or expected from the Thorpe estate, is still pending and is deliberately left out of this note.

What this note does not claim

  • It does not claim Mauch Chunk was the second most popular tourist destination in America. That claim appears on a foundation site and has no independent source in the research log yet, so it is cut.
  • It does not date or retitle any catalog card.
